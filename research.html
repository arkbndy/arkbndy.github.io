Research

Quantum geometry, made material

Wavefunctions carry geometry — Berry curvature, the quantum metric, and the topological invariants built from them. What I find endlessly rewarding is turning that abstract structure into predictions for specific quantum materials: which material, under which field or strain, will show which measurable response. Everything below is one programme seen from five directions, and they talk to each other constantly.

A good deal of it sits on the boundary with chemistry. Close to half of my papers have appeared in chemistry and materials-chemistry journals, and some of the best ideas have come from colleagues who think in ligands, linkers and coordination environments rather than Hamiltonians — which is, I think, exactly where the interesting quantum materials are being made.

What I am building now

At Würzburg I am developing an independent programme on quantum-geometric transport in metal–organic frameworks — lattices whose geometry can be designed chemically, linker by linker, instead of searched for among the crystals that happen to exist. A non-linear Hall effect in metal–organic frameworks, the opening piece of that work, is under review at Physical Review Letters.

The goal is a predictive route from molecular building block to quantum-geometric response — designer quantum materials rather than discovered ones — and it draws on every thread below: the geometry from the first, the exact lattice methods from the second, the materials-prediction machinery from the third, the magnetic and interfacial ingredients from the fourth, and the device questions from the fifth. It is the most exciting thing I have worked on.

01

Quantum geometry & unconventional transport 8 papers · 3 under review

An ordinary Hall voltage needs a magnetic field. A non-linear Hall voltage does not: in a crystal that breaks inversion symmetry, driving a current at frequency ω produces a transverse voltage at 2ω, set entirely by how Berry curvature is distributed across the Fermi surface. The controlling quantity is the Berry curvature dipole, and more recently the quantum metric — the real counterpart of Berry curvature.

My contribution has been to move this from model physics to materials physics. We showed that a gate field switches the sign and size of the Berry curvature dipole in silicene, germanene and stanene, making a quantum-geometric quantity behave like a device parameter; that strain does the same job in layered phosphorene; and that chirality, oxide heterostructure design and metal–organic framework linkers each give an independent handle. That last one is the chemist's knob: swap a linker and you change the lattice, the orbital character and the Berry curvature together, without ever growing a new crystal. The 2024 review in Materials Today Electronics pulled the mechanisms and the candidate materials into one place and has become the standard entry point to the field.

The same thinking extends to anomalous Hall and Nernst responses in magnetic Weyl semimetals, to hidden Berry phases in tellurium nanowires measured by our collaborators in Shanghai, and — most recently and most surprisingly — to a transverse current that comes from nothing more than electron refraction at an interface.

Key papers

  • Non-linear Hall effects: mechanisms and materials. Materials Today Electronics 8, 100101 (2024) — first & corresponding author
  • Electrically switchable giant Berry curvature dipole in silicene, germanene and stanene. 2D Materials 9, 035013 (2022)
  • Refraction-induced transverse charge transport. Phys. Rev. B 114, 105406 (2026) — Editors' Suggestion
02

Flat bands, non-Hermitian topology & exact lattice methods 5 papers · 1 under review

Some lattices are generous: they let you solve them. A recurring pleasure in my work is finding the structure that makes a complicated tight-binding problem exactly tractable — and then reading the physics straight off the answer instead of scanning parameters numerically.

Flat bands are the clearest example. A flat band has no dispersion at all: kinetic energy is quenched, every state costs the same, and interactions decide what happens. Kagome lattices produce them by destructive interference, and with coupled kagome layers we traced the flat bands to their line-graph origin and identified exactly when interlayer coupling gaps them into topologically non-trivial bands. At Würzburg I work with the experimental groups on the minimal two-dimensional multi-orbital kagome model — a case where the lattice has actually been built and photoemission can test the prediction directly. Collaborations like this one, where theory and measurement meet on the same sample, are the ones I enjoy most.

Non-Hermitian systems are the second example, and the same tool cracks them. Real systems leak; once gain and loss are included the Hamiltonian is no longer Hermitian, eigenvalues become complex, and new phenomena appear — exceptional points where eigenvectors coalesce, and the skin effect, where bulk states pile up at a boundary. Real-space decimation eliminates sites exactly, turning stubborn numerics back into clean analytics: we obtained closed-form conditions for flat bands and skin modes in decorated lattices, and located exceptional points analytically in the dice-lattice Haldane model. There is real satisfaction in replacing a numerical scan with a formula that simply tells you the answer.

Key papers

  • Origin of flat bands and non-trivial topology in coupled kagome lattices. Communications Physics 8, 519 (2025) — joint first author
  • Non-Hermitian topology and flat bands via an exact real-space decimation scheme. Phys. Rev. B 110, 085431 (2024) — joint first author
  • Non-Hermiticity induced exceptional points and skin effect in the Haldane model on a dice lattice. Phys. Rev. B 107, 035403 (2023)
03

Dirac materials beyond graphene 19 papers

Graphene's Dirac cones come from the honeycomb lattice, but the honeycomb is not the only way to get them — and finding the others has been a delight. This is the largest body of my work, running from my doctoral research to the present, and it is also the engine for everything else on this page: before you can compute the quantum geometry of a material, someone has to predict the material.

This is as much structural chemistry as band theory. What separates these sheets from graphene is the bonding — sp-hybridised acetylenic links between sp² rings, four- and eight-membered rings in place of hexagons, nitrogen substituted into the network — and the electronic structure follows from that chemistry. It is why so much of this work has appeared in chemistry journals, and why the collaborations behind it are with chemists as often as with physicists.

Highlights include S-graphene, whose two Dirac cones are protected by a tight-binding argument that survives distortion; 8-16-4 graphyne, a square-lattice nodal-line semimetal with a non-trivial Zak index; the dumbbell C₃NX family and its quasi-one-dimensional derivatives; phagraphene under site-specific doping; graphyne quantum dots characterised through their Raman modes; and T-graphene sheets whose bands can be tuned by magnetic flux. Two review articles — in Reports on Progress in Physics and Journal of Physics: Condensed Matter — map the wider landscape, and both have received IOP India Top Cited Paper Awards.

Not all of it is Dirac physics. Several of these sheets are ordinary semiconductors doing interesting things: negative differential resistance in square graphynes, current rectification and carbon-monoxide sensing in tetragonal graphene nanodots, band gaps opened and closed by doping or an electric field. Working out the semiconductor device response from first principles, through Green-function and NEGF transport, is a large part of what this thread does.

The workflow is the same every time: propose a structure, check that it holds together, compute what its electrons do, and ask what makes it worth someone's time in a laboratory. It is how I found most of the quantum materials I now study for their geometry.

Key papers

  • 8-16-4 graphyne: square-lattice 2D nodal-line semimetal with a nontrivial Zak index. Phys. Rev. B 103, 075137 (2021) — first author
  • The topology and robustness of two Dirac cones in S-graphene. Scientific Reports 10, 2502 (2020) — first author
  • A review on role of tetra-rings in the graphene systems. Rep. Prog. Phys. 83, 056501 (2020) — first author; Top Cited Paper Award
04

Magnetism, oxide interfaces & spintronics 3 papers · 2 under review

At the interface between two perovskite oxides the oxygen octahedra do not simply meet — they tilt, and the tilt pattern propagates for several unit cells. That structural detail turns out to control the spin–orbit ground state. Working with a wonderful group of experimentalists combining X-ray spectroscopy, photoemission and transport, we established an unconventional route to correlate octahedral tilt coupling with spin–orbit reconstruction, published in Nature Communications.

The same interface physics feeds two applied threads now under review: antiferromagnetic tunnel barriers that produce discrete tunnelling-magnetoresistance states, and magnon–electromagnon anomalies in rare-earth-doped BiFeO₃ films.

Alongside these sits altermagnetism — a spin order that gives momentum-dependent spin splitting without net magnetisation, and one of the most interesting things to have happened to magnetism in a decade. Our Chemical Society Reviews article recasts it in chemical language, so that the symmetry conditions become rules a materials chemist can apply. Magnetism at the molecular scale belongs here too: with synthetic chemists at the Solid State and Structural Chemistry Unit in Bengaluru we showed how chemical modulation of the ligand field, and the intermolecular interactions in the crystal, switch the spin state of manganese(III) complexes. Quantum materials do not have to be inorganic crystals — some of them are molecules.

Key papers

  • An unconventional pathway to correlate octahedral tilt coupling and spin–orbit reconstruction at oxide interfaces. Nature Communications 17, 332 (2026) — joint first author
  • Altermagnetism from the viewpoint of chemistry. Chem. Soc. Rev. (2026)
  • Spin-state switching in manganese(III) complexes. Dalton Trans. 52, 11335 (2023)
05

Materials for energy & devices 6 papers

A band structure is interesting on its own terms, but it is more interesting when it does something. A steady thread of my work asks what the materials we predict are actually good for — and the answers have been pleasingly practical.

Carbon frameworks that store lithium: a nodal flexible-surface three-dimensional carbon network that works as a lithium-ion anode, chosen as the journal's cover article, and a new two-dimensional carbon system that adsorbs lithium while its isostructural nitrides carry Dirac fermions. MN₄ monolayers that combine an anisotropic Dirac cone with excellent supercapacitive performance. Tetragonal germanene whose field-tuned bands turn into a thermoelectric response, and tetragonal silicene and germanene quantum dots that are strong non-linear optical and photocatalytic candidates. And, in a thoroughly enjoyable collaboration with synthetic chemists, π-conjugated redox polymers whose specific energy density rises when the acceptor conjugation is extended — a case where the design rule came out as a statement about molecular structure rather than about a band.

What ties these together is the workflow used for the fundamental questions — formation energies, band structures, adsorption and charge-transfer energetics — pointed at a device figure of merit instead of a topological invariant. I find it a healthy discipline: it keeps the theory honest about whether anyone would ever build the thing.

Key papers

  • A nodal flexible-surface three-dimensional carbon network as a lithium-ion battery anode. J. Mater. Chem. C 12, 7878 (2024) — journal cover article
  • MN₄ monolayers with anisotropic Dirac cone and excellent supercapacitive performance. Applied Surface Science 702, 163250 (2025)
  • Extended conjugation acceptors increase specific energy densities in π-conjugated redox polymers. J. Phys. Chem. C 127, 5238 (2023)
